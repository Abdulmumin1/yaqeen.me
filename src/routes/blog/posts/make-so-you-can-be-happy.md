*I think people who feel flat after a month of rest were consuming the whole time. Here is why making things fixes it.*

School is the thing forced on you. Summer vacation is the thing that's free. So you grow up believing work is the unnatural state, and that the ideal life is one long vacation: nothing you have to do, all day to be entertained.

Supposedly.

Then you get the month off. You spend it watching, scrolling, playing, one screen after another. It feels like rest while it's happening. At the end you're foggy and restless, and you don't feel rested at all.

I don't think we're built to be passive consumers. The happiness that lasts comes from making things. Here is how I think it works.

**1\. Making makes you meet reality.**

When you consume something, you watch. You can pick apart a product or disagree with a proposal without solving a single problem the creator had to solve. It's very easy to feel clever from the stands. (Sports fans, you know who you are.)

Make something and that stops. The compiler doesn't care about your good intentions. It compiles or it doesn't. The wood doesn't care about your vision either. The legs are even or the table wobbles.

That friction is unpleasant at first, so we avoid it. It's also where the satisfaction lives. Finish a tiny program, or fix the thing in your house that never worked, and you've changed reality a little. There's an object in the world that wasn't there before you sat down. Consuming can't give you that.

Speaking of starting...

**2\. Make small things, and make them yours.**

Many people don't make things because they assume what they make has to matter. If you cook, it should be glamorous. If you code, it should be a startup. This is a trap. Set the bar that high and starting costs so much that you never start. You get stuck in the gap between your taste and your ability.

Good work mostly starts small and a bit silly. Programmers who build great things usually began with a script that automated something annoying, or a toy to see if it would work. The goal isn't to impress anyone. It's to bring into existence something you think is neat.

Watch a child with a pile of bottle caps. He isn't asking whether the little car will ever be a real vehicle. He lines them up, pushes them around, and watches it take shape. Adults lose this, then wonder why they're restless.

Speaking of restless...

**3\. Makers don't get bored.**

If you only consume, the world has to entertain you. When it's dull or irritating, you're stuck with it.

A maker always has a project turning over in the back of their head. In line or waiting for a train, the consumer looks for a screen. The maker is daydreaming about a bug, or about how to arrange the IKEA table.

This is also the cure for helplessness. A lot of modern unhappiness is the feeling that the world is a huge machine run by people far away, and nothing you do matters. Making things at human scale is a reminder that you're not just a passenger.

**Try it.**

If you're unhappy and don't know why, compare what you consumed last month to what you produced. For most people with a lingering malaise, the ratio is almost all consumption.

You don't have to quit your job or found a company. Make anything, as long as it's yours:

*   Rebuild something you use every day instead of buying another one.
*   Fix something broken in your house.
*   Write a program that does one small useful thing.
*   Rearrange your room until it feels right.

The point is to stop waiting to be amused and start leaning in to shape things. If you want to be happy, make something.
